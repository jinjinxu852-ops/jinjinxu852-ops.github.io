Recommended Peptide Purity

Ordering a higher purity than your experiment needs is the easiest way to overspend on peptides. Ordering too low is the easiest way to waste a month. This guide matches the two.

Request a Quote See price list

Crude to >70%

Screening, peptide arrays, first-pass work.

>75% to >85%

Antibody production, epitope mapping, ELISA.

>90% to >95%

Cell assays, enzyme kinetics, binding studies.

>98%

In vivo work, NMR, crystallography, standards.

Purity levels and what they suit

Crude (no purification). The peptide as it comes off the resin after cleavage, desalted at most. Contains truncated sequences and residual reagents. Appropriate for peptide arrays, mass spectrometry standards where only the correct mass matters, and very large library screens where cost per peptide dominates.

>70% to >75%. Enough for immunisation to raise polyclonal antibodies, because the immune system responds to the dominant species. Also used for first-pass binding screens.

>80% to >85%. The workhorse grade for ELISA, epitope mapping, Western blot controls and enzyme substrate screening. A good default when the assay is qualitative or semi-quantitative.

>90% to >95%. Needed when the number matters: cell-based bioassays, receptor binding studies, IC50 and Kd determinations, monoclonal antibody production, and any work where an impurity could produce its own signal.

>98%. For in vivo studies, NMR and crystallography, analytical reference standards, and work intended to support a regulatory submission. Also appropriate for isotope-labelled internal standards.

Quick reference by application

Application Suggested purity
Peptide arrays, large crude libraries Crude
Polyclonal antibody production (immunogen) >75%
ELISA, epitope mapping, dot blot >85%
Enzyme substrate screening >85%
Monoclonal antibody production >90%
Cell-based bioassays >95%
Receptor-ligand binding, Kd / IC50 >95%
Mass spectrometry internal standards >95% to >98%
In vivo and animal studies >98%
NMR, crystallography >98%

These are starting points. If your assay is unusually sensitive, go one step higher.

Two things purity does not cover

How much peptide is in the vial. Purity ignores water and counter-ions entirely. A >98% pure peptide can still be only 65% peptide by weight. If you need an accurate concentration, add net peptide content analysis.

What the counter-ion is. Standard peptides are TFA salts, which is a problem for cell culture at higher concentrations. See TFA and acetate content.

Frequently asked questions

What does >95% peptide purity actually mean?

It means the main peak accounts for at least 95% of the total peak area in an analytical HPLC run with UV detection at 220 nm. The remaining 5% is other peptide-related species. It says nothing about water or salt content, which is measured separately as net peptide content.

Is higher purity always better?

Only if the experiment needs it. Each purity step above >95% costs significantly more and consumes more material during purification, and for screening work the extra purity changes nothing. Buy the grade that fits the assay.

Can I screen with crude peptide and purify later?

Yes, and for library work this is the sensible approach: screen crude material, then resynthesise the hits at high purity for follow-up. Just be aware that crude peptides carry residual reagents that can interfere with cell-based assays.

Does purity affect how long a peptide lasts?

Indirectly. Impurities can include reactive species that accelerate degradation, so a purer peptide often stores better. Storage conditions matter more, though — see our handling and storage guide.
