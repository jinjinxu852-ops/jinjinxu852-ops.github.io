Handling & Storage

A peptide that arrives in perfect condition can be worthless a month later. Three things destroy them — moisture, repeated freeze-thaw cycles and oxidation — and all three are avoidable.

Storing lyophilised peptides

This is the stable form, and peptides should be kept in it for as long as possible.

  • -20°C for peptides in regular use
  • -80°C for long-term storage and for valuable or sensitive material
  • Keep the vial sealed, in the dark, with desiccant where available
  • Always warm to room temperature before opening. Cold glass condenses atmospheric water onto the powder, and moisture is the main cause of slow degradation
  • Reseal promptly; minimise the number of times the vial is opened

Storing peptides in solution

Solution is where peptides degrade. Treat it as a working state, not a storage state.

  • Prepare a concentrated stock rather than a dilute working solution
  • Divide into single-use aliquots immediately, before any freezing
  • Freeze at -20°C, or -80°C for longer periods
  • Thaw an aliquot, use it, discard the remainder — never refreeze
  • Use sterile, low-binding tubes; peptides adsorb to ordinary plastic, and at low concentration the loss is significant
  • Buffer near neutral pH where the sequence allows; strongly alkaline conditions accelerate several degradation routes

Sequences that need extra care

Residue or motif Risk Precaution
Cysteine Oxidation, disulfide scrambling Degassed buffer, slightly acidic pH, no DMSO
Methionine Oxidation to the sulfoxide Avoid DMSO and prolonged air exposure
Tryptophan Oxidation, light sensitivity Store dark, avoid oxidising conditions
Asparagine, glutamine Deamidation Keep near neutral pH, store frozen
Asp-Gly Aspartimide formation Avoid alkaline conditions
N-terminal glutamine Cyclisation to pyroglutamate Store frozen, avoid long periods in solution
Fluorescent labels Photobleaching Amber vials, minimise light exposure

Shipping and receiving

Peptides ship at ambient temperature as lyophilised powder; brief warmth during transit does not harm dry material. On arrival, transfer to the freezer promptly, and if the vial feels cold, let it equilibrate before opening.

Check the vial contents against the certificate of analysis when it arrives. Small quantities of peptide can be almost invisible at the bottom of a vial — centrifuge briefly before concluding it is empty.

When you suspect degradation

If an assay stops working and the peptide is the suspect, an HPLC and MS check will show whether the material is intact. The mass tells the story: +16 Da points to oxidation, +1 Da to deamidation, −17 Da to pyroglutamate formation at the N-terminus.

Frequently asked questions

How long can I store a peptide?

Stored lyophilised at -20°C or below, protected from moisture, most peptides remain usable for years. In solution the useful life is far shorter — days to weeks at 4°C, longer frozen in aliquots, and shorter still for sequences with sensitive residues.

Can I store peptides in the fridge instead of the freezer?

For short-term use over a few days, 4°C is acceptable for lyophilised material. For anything longer, use a freezer. Repeated warming and cooling in a frost-free freezer is also harmful, so prefer a manual-defrost unit if you have one.

Why should I not refreeze a thawed aliquot?

Each freeze-thaw cycle concentrates solutes, shifts local pH and promotes aggregation, and the damage accumulates. Aliquoting once into single-use volumes avoids the problem entirely.

My peptide was fine last month and now gives no signal. What happened?

The most common causes are moisture uptake from repeated opening of a cold vial, oxidation of methionine or cysteine in solution, and aggregation after several freeze-thaw cycles. An HPLC and MS check will usually show which.
