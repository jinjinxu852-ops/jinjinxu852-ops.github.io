Peptide Content (Elemental Analysis)

The vial contains peptide, water and counter-ions. If you weigh the powder and assume it is all peptide, your concentration is wrong — often by thirty per cent or more.

Request a Quote See price list

What it measures

The percentage of the powder that is peptide.

Typical range

Commonly 50–90% depending on sequence and salt form.

Methods

Amino acid analysis, or nitrogen by elemental analysis.

Who needs it

Anyone dosing by concentration, or quantifying by MS.

Why the weight in the vial is not the peptide

A lyophilised peptide is hygroscopic: it picks up water from the air, and some stays bound even after drying. Synthesis and purification leave counter-ions, usually trifluoroacetate, paired with every basic residue. Both add weight.

For a peptide with several arginines or lysines, counter-ions alone can account for a fifth of the mass. Add bound water, and a sample that is 98% pure by HPLC may be only two-thirds peptide by weight.

If your experiment calculates concentration from the weighed mass, that gap goes straight into your result — and because it differs between lots, the error is not even consistent.

How we measure it

Amino acid analysis. The peptide is hydrolysed completely into its constituent amino acids, which are then separated and quantified against known standards. Summing them gives the absolute amount of peptide in the sample, and the ratio to the weighed mass gives net peptide content. This is the reference method, and it has the useful side effect of confirming the amino acid composition.

Elemental analysis. Total nitrogen in the sample is measured and compared with the nitrogen content calculated from the sequence. Faster and less expensive than amino acid analysis, though it assumes no other nitrogen-containing material is present.

We will recommend which method suits your sample and budget.

Reading the result

A certificate stating 72% net peptide content means that 10 mg of powder contains 7.2 mg of peptide. To prepare a 1 mM solution, base the calculation on 7.2 mg, not 10 mg.

Combine this with counter-ion analysis for the full picture: content tells you how much peptide there is, counter-ion analysis tells you what makes up the rest.

When to order

Order content analysis with the synthesis, not afterwards. The test is destructive, and sacrificing several milligrams of an expensive peptide later is a poor trade. It is routinely worth it for:

  • In vivo and cell-based dosing
  • Quantitative binding and enzyme kinetics
  • Isotope-labelled internal standards for mass spectrometry
  • Reference standards and any material used across multiple studies

What you receive

  • Net peptide content as a percentage
  • The method used and the conditions
  • For amino acid analysis, the measured composition against the theoretical values
  • Guidance on calculating concentrations, also covered in our reconstitution procedure

Frequently asked questions

What is net peptide content?

The proportion of a lyophilised sample that is peptide, as opposed to bound water, counter-ions such as trifluoroacetate or acetate, and residual salts. It is usually between 50% and 90%, so a vial labelled 10 mg may contain only 7 mg of actual peptide.

How is it different from HPLC purity?

Purity compares the peptide with other peptide species; it ignores water and salt entirely. A peptide can be 98% pure by HPLC and still be only 65% peptide by weight. The two numbers answer different questions and you often need both.

When do I really need this test?

Whenever an absolute concentration matters: in vivo dosing, quantitative binding measurements such as Kd or IC50, isotope-labelled internal standards, and any assay where results must be comparable between peptide lots.

How much sample does the test consume?

Amino acid analysis is destructive and uses roughly 1 mg. Because it cannot be recovered, order content analysis as part of the original synthesis rather than sacrificing precious material later.
