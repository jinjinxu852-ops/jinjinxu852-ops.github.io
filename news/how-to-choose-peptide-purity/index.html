How to choose the right peptide purity for your experiment

Of all the decisions on a peptide order form, purity is the one that most often costs customers money for no return. Ordering >98% when the experiment would run perfectly on >85% can double the price and add a week to the delivery time, and the extra purity changes nothing about the result.

The opposite mistake is worse. A peptide that is too impure for the assay produces a result you cannot interpret, and you will not know that until the experiment has already consumed a month.

What the purity number actually measures

When a certificate of analysis says >95%, it refers to one specific measurement: the area of the main peak in an analytical HPLC run, as a percentage of the total peak area, with UV detection at 220 nm.

That wavelength matters. At 220 nm the peptide bond itself absorbs, so every peptide species in the sample is counted. Detection at 280 nm would only see tryptophan, tyrosine and phenylalanine, and would miss any impurity that lacks them.

The remaining few per cent is other peptide-related material: truncated sequences from incomplete couplings, oxidised species, partially deprotected molecules. These are the impurities most likely to interfere with your assay, because they are chemically similar to your peptide.

What the purity number does not measure

Here is where orders go wrong. Purity says nothing about how much peptide is in the vial.

A lyophilised peptide is peptide plus bound water plus counter-ions — usually trifluoroacetate, one for each basic residue. A sequence with several arginines can easily be 30% counter-ion and water by weight while still being 98% pure by HPLC. The two numbers answer completely different questions:

  • Purity: of the peptide material present, what fraction is the target sequence?
  • Net peptide content: of the powder you weighed, what fraction is peptide at all?

If your experiment calculates a concentration from the weighed mass, you need net peptide content, not purity. Skipping it means every concentration in your study is wrong by the same unknown margin — and because the margin differs between lots, your results are not even internally consistent.

Matching purity to the experiment

Crude to >70%. The peptide as cleaved, desalted at most. Fine for peptide arrays and very large screens where the cost per peptide dominates and a qualitative answer is all you need.

>75% to >85%. Enough for polyclonal antibody production — the immune system responds to the dominant species — and for ELISA, epitope mapping and substrate screening. This is the right default for a great deal of routine work.

>90% to >95%. Needed once numbers matter: cell-based bioassays, receptor binding, IC50 and Kd determinations, monoclonal antibody work. At this level you can be confident that an observed effect comes from your peptide rather than from something alongside it.

>98%. For in vivo studies, NMR and crystallography, analytical reference standards and anything supporting a regulatory submission.

Three practical rules

Screen cheap, confirm expensive. Run the first pass on crude or moderately pure material, then resynthesise only the hits at high purity. For library work this is standard practice, and it saves a great deal.

Watch the counter-ion in cell culture. Residual TFA is cytotoxic, and at the concentrations used in some assays there is enough of it to affect viability. If cells behave strangely, run a vehicle control containing the same counter-ion without peptide before blaming the peptide. Salt exchange to acetate solves it properly.

Describe the application, not just the grade. When you request a quote, say what the peptide is for. We will recommend a purity — including, when it applies, a lower one than you asked for.

Summary

Purity is a measurement of one thing, not a general quality score. Match it to what your assay can tolerate, measure peptide content separately when concentrations matter, and keep the counter-ion in mind for cell work. Our recommended peptide purity guide lists more applications in table form.
