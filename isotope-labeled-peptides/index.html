Isotope Labeling

A heavy-labelled peptide behaves chemically like the natural one but weighs more, so a mass spectrometer can tell them apart. That makes it the internal standard for absolute quantitation.

What stable isotope labelling means

One or more amino acids in the sequence are replaced by versions in which ordinary carbon-12 or nitrogen-14 atoms are substituted by the heavier, non-radioactive isotopes ¹³C and ¹⁵N. Nothing about the chemistry changes: the labelled peptide has the same sequence, the same retention time and the same ionisation behaviour. Only the mass is different.

That difference is what makes it useful. In a single injection, the mass spectrometer sees both your sample peptide and the known amount of heavy standard you spiked in, and the ratio of their signals gives the concentration directly.

Labelling options

  • Single labelled residue. One amino acid in the sequence is heavy, typically at the C-terminus for tryptic peptides. The usual choice for quantitative assays.
  • Multiple labelled residues. Several positions are labelled when a larger mass shift is needed to avoid overlap with the natural isotope envelope.
  • Uniform labelling. Every residue is labelled, used mainly in NMR work.
  • ¹⁵N only or ¹³C only. Available when the experiment requires a specific nuclide.

Heavy Leu, Lys, Arg, Val, Phe, Ala, Gly, Ile and Pro are routinely available. Other labelled building blocks can be sourced; send the residue and the label pattern and we will confirm availability and cost.

Applications

  • Targeted proteomics. Internal standards for SRM, MRM and PRM assays measuring specific proteins in complex samples.
  • Absolute protein quantitation. AQUA-style workflows, where a known amount of heavy peptide is spiked into the digest.
  • Pharmacokinetics. Measuring a therapeutic peptide in plasma against its heavy twin.
  • NMR. Uniformly labelled peptides for structural work.

Specification and data

Isotope-labelled peptides are expensive per milligram, mainly because the labelled building blocks are, so we usually recommend ordering a small quantity at high purity. Standard delivery includes analytical HPLC and mass spectrometry, with isotopic enrichment stated as supplied by the building-block manufacturer.

For quantitative work, add net peptide content analysis: without it, the concentration of your standard is an estimate, and every result calculated from it inherits that error.

Storage

Store lyophilised at -20°C or below. Prepare a concentrated stock, aliquot it into single-use volumes and freeze them; repeated freeze-thaw cycles degrade the standard and shift your calibration over time. See handling and storage.

Frequently asked questions

Why do I need an isotope labeled peptide?

In targeted mass spectrometry you cannot convert a peak area into a concentration without a reference. A heavy peptide of the same sequence co-elutes and ionises like the natural peptide but appears at a different mass, so the ratio of the two peaks gives an absolute amount.

Which residue should carry the heavy label?

For tryptic assays, heavy lysine or arginine at the C-terminus is standard, because every tryptic peptide ends in one of them. Otherwise choose a residue that appears once in the sequence and is not prone to modification — avoid methionine, which oxidises.

How big is the mass shift?

It depends on the labelled residue. A uniformly ¹³C,¹⁵N-labelled lysine adds about 8 Da and arginine about 10 Da, which is enough to separate the heavy and light forms cleanly in the mass spectrometer.

Do I need net peptide content for an internal standard?

Yes, if you want absolute quantitation. The weight in the vial includes water and counter-ions, so weighing alone overestimates how much peptide you have. Net peptide content by amino acid analysis gives the true value.
