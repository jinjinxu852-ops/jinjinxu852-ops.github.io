Routine HPLC & MS Analysis

The standard pair of tests behind every peptide: HPLC to show how pure it is, mass spectrometry to prove it is the right molecule. Included with our peptides and available for samples from elsewhere.

Reversed-phase HPLC

C18 separation with UV detection at 220 nm.

Purity as peak area

Main peak reported as a percentage of total area.

Mass confirmation

Measured molecular weight against the calculated value.

Full report

Chromatogram, spectrum, method and conditions.

Analytical HPLC

The peptide is dissolved and injected onto a reversed-phase column, usually C18. A gradient of increasing organic solvent — acetonitrile in water, both containing a small amount of TFA — gradually releases the peptides from the column, with the least hydrophobic eluting first. A UV detector records absorbance at 220 nm.

The result is a chromatogram in which each species appears as a peak. Purity is calculated as the area of the main peak divided by the total area of all peaks, expressed as a percentage.

What the impurity peaks represent depends on where they appear. Peaks eluting earlier than the main product are often truncated or oxidised species; later peaks may be incompletely deprotected material or aggregated forms.

Mass spectrometry

A portion of the sample is analysed by mass spectrometry and the measured molecular weight is compared with the value calculated from the sequence. This is the identity test: the right mass confirms the right molecule.

When the mass is wrong, the size of the difference is informative:

Mass difference Likely cause
+16 Da Oxidation, usually of methionine
+18 Da Incomplete cyclisation or hydrolysis
−18 Da Water loss, including successful amide cyclisation
−2 Da per bridge Disulfide bond formed
+80 Da Phosphate group present
Residue mass missing Deletion sequence from an incomplete coupling

What the report contains

  • Annotated HPLC chromatogram with the purity calculation
  • Mass spectrum with measured and theoretical molecular weight
  • Column, gradient, flow rate and detection wavelength
  • Sample identification and the date of analysis

When to order this test on a peptide you already have

  • Before a critical experiment, to confirm that stored material is still intact
  • When an assay result changes unexpectedly between peptide lots
  • When a peptide from another supplier does not perform as its certificate suggests
  • Before publication, if your reviewers expect characterisation data

Frequently asked questions

Why is peptide purity measured at 220 nm?

At 220 nm the peptide bond itself absorbs, so every peptide species in the sample is detected regardless of whether it contains an aromatic residue. Detection at 280 nm only sees tryptophan, tyrosine and phenylalanine, and would miss impurities lacking them.

Does a single sharp HPLC peak mean the peptide is pure?

Not necessarily. Species with nearly identical hydrophobicity — some positional isomers, certain deamidation products — can hide under one peak. When that risk matters, LC-MS gives mass information across the peak and can separate what HPLC alone cannot.

Why does my peptide's purity differ from the certificate?

Methods differ. A different column, gradient or detection wavelength can shift the number by a few percent. Larger differences usually mean the peptide has degraded since delivery, often through moisture uptake or oxidation in solution.

Is HPLC purity the same as peptide content?

No, and confusing the two is a common source of error. Purity describes the peptide fraction relative to other peptide species; content describes how much of the weighed powder is peptide rather than water and salt. See peptide content analysis.
