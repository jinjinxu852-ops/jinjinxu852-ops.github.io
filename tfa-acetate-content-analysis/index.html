TFA, Acetate Content Analysis

Residual trifluoroacetate from synthesis and purification is toxic to cells at concentrations that are easy to reach by accident. We measure it, and when it matters, we exchange it for acetate.

Where the TFA comes from

Trifluoroacetic acid appears twice in peptide manufacture: in the cocktail that cleaves the peptide from the resin and removes side-chain protection, and in the mobile phase used for reversed-phase HPLC purification.

By the end, every basic residue — arginine, lysine, histidine, and the free N-terminus — carries a trifluoroacetate counter-ion. This is why peptides are supplied as TFA salts by default, and why the problem scales with the number of basic residues in your sequence.

Why it matters

Cell culture. Trifluoroacetate is cytotoxic. Add a peptide at a high concentration and you may add enough TFA to affect viability, proliferation or differentiation — and attribute the effect to the peptide.

Primary cells and stem cells are more sensitive than established lines, and are exactly where misleading results are most costly.

In vivo studies. Regulatory and ethical considerations aside, TFA is undesirable in anything administered to an animal. Acetate is the standard alternative.

Enzyme assays. Residual TFA can inhibit some enzymes and shift pH in small-volume reactions.

The analysis

We quantify trifluoroacetate, and acetate where relevant, and report both as a percentage of sample weight. Read the result together with net peptide content: together they account for what is in the vial besides peptide.

After a salt exchange, the test serves a second purpose — confirming that TFA has actually gone and acetate has replaced it, rather than assuming it.

Salt exchange

When the measurement shows a problem, or when you know in advance that your assay is sensitive, we exchange the counter-ion:

  • TFA to acetate: the standard choice for cell-based and in vivo work
  • TFA to hydrochloride: where chloride is preferable
  • Other forms: quoted on request

Exchange is most efficient when it is done as part of the original production, so specify the salt form when you request a quote rather than after delivery.

A practical note

If you are troubleshooting an unexplained toxic effect, run a vehicle control that contains the same amount of counter-ion without the peptide. If the control reproduces the effect, the peptide is not the cause. Our reconstitution procedure explains how to set that up.

Frequently asked questions

Why is TFA a problem in my peptide?

Trifluoroacetic acid is used in cleavage and in HPLC purification, and it stays behind paired with basic residues. It is cytotoxic to cultured cells, can inhibit enzymes and may interfere with cell proliferation assays. Primary cells and sensitive cell lines are affected at levels that a standard TFA-salt peptide can easily deliver.

How do I know whether my peptide has a TFA problem?

Unless the certificate states a counter-ion other than TFA, assume it is a TFA salt. If a peptide kills or slows your cells at concentrations where it should be harmless, and a vehicle control does not explain it, residual TFA is a prime suspect. The test settles the question.

What does salt exchange involve?

The peptide is treated so that trifluoroacetate is displaced by the counter-ion you choose, usually acetate, then re-lyophilised. Some peptide is lost in the process, so order a little extra if you need a specific final amount.

Which counter-ion should I choose?

Acetate is the usual choice for cell culture and in vivo work because it is well tolerated. Hydrochloride is an alternative. If your assay is sensitive to chloride or acetate, tell us and we will discuss the options.
