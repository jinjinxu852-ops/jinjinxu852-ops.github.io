Cyclic Peptide Synthesis

Cyclisation locks a peptide into a defined shape and makes it far harder for proteases to digest. We make head-to-tail, side-chain and disulfide-bridged cyclic peptides, including peptides with several bridges.

Request a Quote See price list

Cyclisation types

Head-to-tail, side chain to tail, side chain to side chain, disulfide.

Multiple bridges

Controlled oxidation for two or three disulfide pairs.

Confirmed structure

Mass spectrometry confirms ring closure and bridge count.

Scale

1 mg to kilogram scale, at purity up to >98%.

Types of cyclisation we offer

Head-to-tail (backbone) cyclisation. The N-terminus is joined to the C-terminus, giving a peptide with no free ends at all. The most protease-resistant option, but the hardest to close for short rings.

Side chain to side chain. An amide bridge between a lysine and an aspartate or glutamate side chain, leaving the termini free for labelling or further modification. Lactam bridges of this type are common for stabilising helices.

Side chain to terminus. A bridge between a side chain and either the N- or C-terminus, used when the ring must span a specific part of the sequence.

Disulfide bridges. Two cysteine residues are oxidised to form an S-S bond. The most common cyclisation in nature, and the usual choice when reproducing a natural peptide such as a toxin or hormone.

Peptides with several disulfide bridges

When a peptide contains four or six cysteines, letting them oxidise freely gives a mixture of isomers with the wrong pairings. We prevent that by protecting the cysteine pairs with different, orthogonally removable groups. Each pair is then deprotected and oxidised in a defined order, so the bridges form where you specify.

Tell us which cysteines pair with which when you order. If the pairing is unknown, we can discuss whether random oxidation followed by analysis is a sensible first step.

What we need to quote a cyclic peptide

  • The sequence, with the cyclisation points marked
  • The type of bridge: amide or disulfide
  • For multiple disulfides, the intended pairing
  • Quantity and purity
  • Any additional modification, such as a fluorescent label

Quality control for cyclic peptides

Cyclic peptides are released on the same basis as linear ones: analytical HPLC for purity and mass spectrometry for identity. The mass shift is itself the evidence of successful closure, since each amide bond formed loses water and each disulfide loses two hydrogens. For peptides with multiple bridges, further analysis to confirm the pairing pattern can be arranged through our peptide analysis services.

Handling cyclic peptides

Disulfide-bridged peptides are sensitive to reducing agents. Keep DTT, TCEP and 2-mercaptoethanol out of buffers used with them, and avoid prolonged exposure to alkaline pH, which promotes scrambling. See handling and storage for details.

Frequently asked questions

Why make a cyclic peptide instead of a linear one?

Cyclisation reduces the number of conformations the peptide can adopt, which often raises affinity for the target, and it removes the free termini that exopeptidases attack, which extends half-life in serum and in cells.

How do you confirm the peptide actually cyclised?

Cyclisation changes the molecular weight in a predictable way — loss of water for an amide bond, loss of two hydrogens for each disulfide bridge. Mass spectrometry shows this shift, and HPLC shows the cyclic product as a distinct peak from the linear precursor.

Can you make a peptide with more than one disulfide bridge?

Yes. Peptides with two or three bridges are made using orthogonal cysteine protecting groups, so the pairs are formed one at a time in a controlled order rather than left to oxidise at random.

Is a cyclic peptide more expensive than a linear one?

Yes. Cyclisation adds reaction and purification steps, and multiple disulfide bridges add more. The exact difference depends on ring size and bridge count and is stated in the quote.
