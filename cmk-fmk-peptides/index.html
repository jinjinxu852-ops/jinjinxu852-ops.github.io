CMK, FMK Modification

A reactive warhead at the C-terminus turns a peptide into a covalent inhibitor of the protease that recognises it. We make both chloromethyl ketone and fluoromethyl ketone versions to your sequence.

Chloromethyl ketone, highly reactive, serine and cysteine proteases.

Fluoromethyl ketone, more selective, widely used for caspases.

How these peptides work

A protease recognises a short sequence around the bond it cuts. If that recognition sequence is synthesised with a reactive group in place of the normal C-terminal carboxyl, the enzyme binds the peptide as if it were a substrate, and the warhead then forms a permanent covalent bond with the catalytic residue. The protease is inactivated and stays that way.

Because the bond is irreversible, these reagents are used both to switch a protease off in an experiment and to tag active enzyme molecules so they can be detected.

CMK versus FMK

Chloromethyl ketone (CMK) is the more reactive warhead. It alkylates the active-site histidine of serine proteases and the cysteine of cysteine proteases, acting quickly and at low concentration. The same reactivity makes it less discriminating, so CMK peptides are better suited to purified enzyme work than to living cells.

Fluoromethyl ketone (FMK) reacts more slowly and more selectively, and is generally better tolerated by cells. FMK peptides are the standard tool for inhibiting caspases in apoptosis experiments, where broad-spectrum and caspase-selective versions are both used.

What we can make

  • CMK or FMK peptides on any recognition sequence you specify
  • N-terminally capped versions, commonly with a Z (benzyloxycarbonyl) or acetyl group, to block exopeptidase attack
  • Probes combining the warhead with biotin for streptavidin capture, or with a fluorescent dye for in-gel detection of labelled proteases
  • Unmodified control peptides lacking the warhead, for comparison

Choosing the recognition sequence

If you know the preferred cleavage motif of your protease, send it and we will place the warhead on it. If you do not, a peptide library screen can identify the preferred substrate sequence first, and the warhead can then be built onto the winner.

Delivery and data

CMK and FMK peptides are supplied lyophilised with analytical HPLC and mass spectrometry data. Because the warhead hydrolyses in water, these peptides are purified and handled under conditions that keep exposure to aqueous solution short; this sometimes limits the maximum purity achievable, and we state the realistic target in the quote.

Safety documentation is supplied with each order. These are reactive compounds — read it before opening the vial.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between CMK and FMK peptides?

Both attach covalently to the catalytic residue of a protease, but the chloromethyl ketone is more chemically reactive and therefore less selective, while the fluoromethyl ketone reacts more slowly and tends to be more specific in cells. FMK inhibitors such as Z-VAD-FMK are the standard choice in caspase work.

Are these peptides selective for one protease?

Selectivity comes from the recognition sequence, not the warhead. A well-chosen sequence directs the inhibitor to its target, but off-target reaction with other proteases is always possible, especially with CMK. Always include an appropriate control in cell-based experiments.

How should CMK and FMK peptides be dissolved and stored?

Dissolve in anhydrous DMSO, aliquot, and store at -20°C or below protected from moisture. The warhead is hydrolysed by water, so avoid aqueous stock solutions and do not leave the material at room temperature in solution.

Can these peptides be used safely?

They are reactive alkylating compounds. Handle them with gloves in a fume hood, follow your institution's rules for reactive chemicals, and dispose of them as hazardous waste. Request the safety information with your order.
